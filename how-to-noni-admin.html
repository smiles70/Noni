1. Signing in to the admin console

1
Open https://www.mynaani.com/admin in any browser. You will see the mynaani logo with a Username field, a Password field, and a Log in button.
2
Enter your staff username and the staff password, then click Log in. Usernames are not case-sensitive — Kim, KIM, and kim all work.
3
The console opens. You are signed in for up to 8 hours; after that you will be asked to sign in again. Use the Sign out button (top right) when you finish, especially on a shared computer.
This login is separate from the learner sign-in. It is for employees only — no magic link, no payment account, nothing to do with Stripe.
If the page shows an error like “username or password didn't match”, check spelling and try again. After 5 tries in a minute the page pauses sign-ins for that minute — this is a security brake, not a bug.

2. Onboarding a company or community

Onboarding a customer means creating their organization, attaching a license (how many seats), generating access codes, and giving the customer a link to redeem them.

1
Open the wizard. From Overview or Organizations, click + New organization.
2
Details + location. Name, type (nonprofit, for-profit / senior living, health plan), tier, community size — plus street, city, state, ZIP, and phone for the facility.
3
Contacts. Add one or more named contacts (name, email, phone) and mark the primary one — the person you send codes to.
4
License & go-live. Set seats, the license end date, and the slug (e.g. oaktown-seniors → mynaani.com/for/oaktown-seniors), then Create organization. The console creates the org, attaches the license, generates one access code per seat, and shows a summary with the link and codes to copy.
5
Learners redeem. Each learner opens the link, signs in with their own email, enters their code, and the seat is theirs. Watch seats used fill up on the org's detail page.
Every onboarding action is written to the audit log with your username — kim and steven are recorded as separate people even though you share one password.

3. Managing seats and licenses

  • Seats used vs seats total — visible on every org row in search results and in the org detail page.
  • Expiring licenses — the org detail page shows license expiry dates; the dashboard flags licenses nearing expiry so a renewal reminder can go out before learners lose access.
  • Renewal — create a new license on the same org; seats and learner access continue without interruption.
  • Soft de-provisioning — when a license ends, learner access is removed gently (data is kept), not hard-deleted.

4. Finding organizations and accounts

Organization search

Type at least 3 characters into the Organizations search box. It matches name, contact email, and slug. Each result shows status, type, tier, slug, and seats used / total. Click an org to open its detail page: licenses, how many codes have been issued, and recent audit entries.

Account search (support)

The Accounts search finds a learner by email or display name and shows their account id, whether the account is scheduled for deletion, and how many purchases they have — enough for support questions like “did my purchase go through?”

5. Reports — the organization dashboard

Open an organization, then open its dashboard. This is the reporting view an enterprise customer (or you) sees.

Report elementWhat it tells you
LicensesActive licenses, seat totals, expiry dates
Seats used / remainingAdoption of the purchased seats
Codes issued / redeemedHow many invitations are still outstanding
Aggregate engagementCourse-start and progress rates across the cohort — only shown when at least 5 learners are enrolled (privacy floor)
Child organizationsRollup for portfolio/parent orgs with sub-sites
Audit trailWho changed what, and when

There is no separate “generate report” button — the dashboard is the report. If a customer asks for a written export, that is a known gap to raise with engineering rather than something to improvise with screenshots of learner data.

6. Reviewing flags

The Flags list shows sharing signals raised by the system — for example, an access code pattern suggesting account sharing. Review each flag, look at the account detail, and act according to the support playbook. Flags are a review queue, not an automatic punishment — a human decides.

7. End-to-end scenarios

Real tasks, start to finish, using only the console.

Scenario 1 — A new senior care facility signs up

“Sunrise Gardens Senior Living wants 25 seats for their residents.”

1
Sign in at mynaani.com/admin.
2
In Organizations, search “Sunrise” first — the org may already exist from a sales conversation.
3
Create the organization: name Sunrise Gardens, contact email (their activities director), type senior_living, community size, tier.
4
Attach a license: 25 seats, expiry one year out.
5
Generate 25 access codes.
6
Set the slug sunrise-gardens → their page is mynaani.com/for/sunrise-gardens.
7
Send the contact the link and the codes. Done — watch seats fill on the org page.

Scenario 2 — A facility adds a second building

“Sunrise Gardens opened a second location across town.”

1
Create the new organization for the second site, and set its parent org to Sunrise Gardens.
2
Attach its own license and codes — each site has its own seats.
3
The parent org's dashboard now rolls up both sites automatically — no extra wiring.

Scenario 3 — A learner says “my code doesn't work”

1
Open the org, check codes issued vs redeemed — is the code still unused?
2
Search the learner's email in Accounts — did they already redeem a code under a different email?
3
If the seat count is exhausted, issue more codes or raise the license seat total.

Scenario 4 — Renewal season

1
Open each org's dashboard — licenses nearing expiry are flagged.
2
On renewal, create a new license on the same org — learners keep access without interruption.
3
If they don't renew, the license expires and access de-provisions gently — data is kept, not deleted.

Scenario 5 — A flag appears: suspected code sharing

1
Open the Flags list, read the signal detail and the account row.
2
Decide as a human — a flag is a prompt to review, not an automatic penalty. Contact the org contact first; a facility may simply be onboarding a group session.

Scenario 6 — Weekly ops review

1
Scan Flags — anything new?
2
Spot-check orgs' seats used / total — customers nearing capacity deserve a heads-up before they hit the wall.
3
Check licenses expiring in the next 30 days and queue renewal outreach.
4
Glance at the audit trail on busy orgs to confirm every change has a named actor.

8. The privacy boundary — what you can never see

  • You can never see an individual learner's lesson progress, quiz answers, or confidence scores — not through the console, not through the API. That boundary is built into the code, not just policy.
  • Engagement numbers are aggregate-only and suppressed entirely under 5 learners, so no one can be singled out.
  • Staff actions are audit-logged. Treat the console like cash handling: only open what you need for the task.
  • Never share your login. If a password needs to change, tell engineering — it is rotated as a secret, not sent around.
